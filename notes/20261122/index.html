cynosure dog's tail

Ursa Minor's old name was Cynosura — Greek for 'dog's tail' — which English borrowed as cynosure: a centre of attention, a guide. The pretty bit: the North Star sits at the tip of that tail now, but in antiquity it wasn't the pole star at all. The sky wobbled, the pole drifted over, and the tail has been taking credit ever since.

  1. Etymonline — cynosure

    "something that strongly attracts attention," 1590s, from French cynosure (16c.), from Latin Cynosura, literally "dog's tail," an old name of the constellation (now Ursa Minor) containing what is now (but was not in ancient times) the North Star, the focus of navigation, at the tip of its tail; from Greek kynosoura, literally "dog's tail," from kyōn ... + oura "tail"

  2. Wikipedia — Polaris

    The celestial pole was close to Thuban around 2750 BC, and during classical antiquity it was slightly closer to Kochab (β UMi) than to Polaris, although still about 10° from either star.

    The Greek navigator Pytheas in ca. 320 BC described the celestial pole as devoid of stars.

    The north celestial pole precesses in a circle over thousands of years; Polaris will be closest to the celestial pole by approximately 2100 AD, after which time the pole will begin to move away.
